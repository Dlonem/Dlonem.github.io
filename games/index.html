Buying keys without getting burned

The cheap price is real. So are the four ways it goes wrong. None of this is complicated, but all of it is on you rather than the store.

A marketplace is not a shop

G2A doesn't sell keys. Third-party sellers do, and the marketplace sits in the middle taking a cut. That's the whole thing in one sentence, and every other point below follows from it — you're buying from a stranger with a rating, not from a storefront holding stock.

Check the region before you pay

A key can be locked to accounts registered in a particular country. Very often the reason a key is unusually cheap is the region lock. Listings say which region they're for. A key you can't activate where you live is worth exactly nothing, and finding out afterwards is the most common way people get stung.

"Key" doesn't mean Steam

The same game turns up as a Steam key, a Steam gift, an EA App key, a Ubisoft Connect key or a GOG key — and they land in different launchers. Battlefront II below is the clearest example: the marketplace stock is usually EA App, so if you want it sitting in your Steam library, you want a Steam sale instead.

Buy the key, not the account

Some listings sell you an account with the game on it rather than a key for your own. Don't. The game lives on someone else's login forever, you can't move it to your library, and you're one password reset away from owning nothing. The only time it makes any sense is a bulk library with thousands of titles on it, and that isn't what you're here for. If the listing doesn't clearly say key, close it.

Seller rating is the actual product

Sort by rating and sales volume, not by price alone. The few dollars between the cheapest listing and a seller with thousands of clean sales is the cheapest insurance you will ever buy. If the only thing a listing has going for it is the number, close the tab.

And the part most affiliate pages leave out: a resold key doesn't pay a developer the way a first-hand sale does. For a small studio — Manor Lords is one person's studio, Bellwright is a small team — buy it direct and let them have the money. Marketplaces make sense for big-publisher back catalogue, deep discounts on games that have already made their money, and picking up something you'd otherwise never have bought at all. That's the honest line, and it's where I actually use them.

When to skip all of this: brand-new releases (marketplace pricing isn't better yet, and the risk is highest), anything under about ten dollars (the savings don't cover the hassle), and any game where a regular Steam sale gets you within a few dollars — Steam's own refund window is worth more than the difference.

Common questions

Before you spend anything

Is buying game keys from a marketplace safe?

It can be, but a marketplace is not a store. G2A doesn't sell keys itself — third-party sellers do, and the marketplace sits in the middle. Seller reputation, region lock and key type are all things you check, not things the platform guarantees for you. For a brand-new release or a small indie game, buying direct from Steam or the developer is usually the better call.

What is a region-locked key?

A key that will only activate on an account registered in a particular country or region. Listings state the region, and very often a key is cheap precisely because it's locked somewhere you aren't. Check it before you pay — a key you can't activate is worth nothing, and this is the single most common way people lose money here.

Will a cheap key always end up in my Steam library?

No. The same game can be sold as a Steam key, a Steam gift, an EA App key, a Ubisoft Connect key or a GOG key, and they land in different launchers. Star Wars Battlefront II above is the clean example — the marketplace stock is usually EA App, so I point at a Steam sale for that one instead of taking the commission.

Should I buy an account instead of a key?

No. An account listing hands you someone else's login with the game sitting on it — it never becomes yours, it can't move to your library, and a password reset ends it. Keys only. The single exception is a bulk library of thousands of games, which is a different hobby entirely. I go through this on camera along with the rest of the checks.

What about Steam gifts rather than keys?

A Steam gift is a copy sent to you through Steam itself rather than a code you redeem. It works, and it's a real option when no key exists for the game you want — it's just not my first choice, because it adds a friend request and a waiting step to what should be a thirty-second transaction. I recorded a full gift purchase start to finish so you can see exactly what it looks like before you commit to one.

Should I ever just pay full price?

Yes, and I'd rather say so than pretend otherwise. For games from small studios, buying direct means the developer actually gets paid — a resold key doesn't send them what a first-hand sale does. And where a normal Steam sale gets you within a few dollars, take the sale: Steam's own refund window is worth more than the difference.

Because I buy games this way anyway and the commission costs you nothing. It funds the apps, the mods and the hosting for this site, all of which are free. The moment a link would send you somewhere worse than Steam, I say so on the card instead — there are a couple of those above.
