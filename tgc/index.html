Est. May 2016

The Game Center

The first server I ever made, and the one that turned into a community by accident. It is still running.

How it started

I wanted to be the one who owned it

In May 2016 my friend group was still calling each other on Skype, which meant somebody had to start the call and everybody had to be there for it. I found Discord before most people had heard of it, showed everyone, and they took to it immediately — you just walked into the room, and whoever was there was there.

I will be honest about the next part, because it is the actual reason. I made the server because I wanted to be the one who owned it. I could see where this was going and I wanted to get there first. That is not a noble founding story, but it is the true one.

So The Game Center started as a room for fewer than fifty people to play Dota in, with a plain controller icon and no ambitions. Within about a year there were two hundred and fifty of us, and it kept going from there. There was never a plan or a target. People brought friends, those friends brought friends, and at some point it stopped being a group chat and started being a community.

The first website

Eight characters you cannot copy

Here is a problem that sounds trivial and was not. You cannot copy and paste out of a video game. If you were in a match with someone and wanted to invite them, you had to read eight random characters out loud and hope they typed them correctly.

So I built a website. One page, one button. Go to the address, click join, you are in the room. That was the whole product, and it worked.

I was not a web developer and it showed — it was a WordPress site held together with enthusiasm, and it is long gone now. The idea was right, though. This page is that same idea done properly, nine years later. Nearly everything else I have built since grew out of solving that one small annoyance.

The point of it

Especially the quiet ones

Somewhere in 2017 I wrote myself a short set of messages to send to people who had joined and then never said anything. It was not a moderation thing. I just did not like the idea of somebody sitting in a server for months feeling like they were not really part of it.

One of those messages was written specifically for people who do not talk because they are shy, or because walking into a room where everyone already knows each other is genuinely hard. The gist of it was that the regulars are good people, we post nonsense every single day, and not knowing anyone yet should not be the thing that stops you.

Nine years later that is still the whole idea. I wanted the bar where everybody knows your name, except with a few thousand people in it and considerably more Dota.

The hard part

What it costs to keep a room comfortable

Wanting a place where quiet people feel welcome sounds simple right up until you have to enforce it.

It started with slurs. That one was easy — we do not do that here, and nobody serious argued the point. Then it was people telling each other to go kill themselves, and that is where I stopped treating any of it as banter. I brought in bots that caught those phrases and deleted the message outright, and sometimes removed the person with it.

Then came the pushback: too much censorship. I never agreed, and I still do not. What actually got removed was a short list of things whose only purpose is to make somebody feel small. Everything else was fair game. We eventually opened an uncensored channel where anyone could say whatever they liked, and almost nobody used it — which told me most of the objection was never really about wanting to say those things.

I think that trade was the right one. A server where the loudest people set the rules is a server the quiet ones quietly leave. Keeping it comfortable for the people who had not spoken yet was worth the friction with the people who never stopped.

Staff

It only exists because I sleep

For a long time moderation was just me. People would join specifically to cause problems, I would remove them, and that was the end of it.

The trouble is that I sleep. Things would happen overnight that nobody was awake for, and I would find the wreckage in the morning. That is the only reason staff ever existed — not a hierarchy anybody wanted, just the fact that I live in one time zone. A few people in Europe were awake when I was not, and they became the overnight shift by default.

It took me an absurdly long time to hand anyone the keys.

Game nights

Run badly, remembered fondly

We ran Dota 2 in-house tournament nights. The format was ambitious: twelve against twelve, any rank welcome, a separate voice channel per team, a fallback to six a side if not enough people turned up, and a fallback to just playing a ranked match together if that did not work either.

In hindsight they were held together with tape. I was organising instead of playing, the teams were assembled largely on vibes, and I am still faintly amazed they happened at all given how many people showed up. They were also some of the best nights the server ever had. Both of those things are true at once.

2018 – 2021

The Christmas raffles

For four Decembers running I did holiday giveaways — Game Center mugs, shirts, stickers you could stick on the side of a desktop, and a pile of games bought out of my own pocket. There was no sponsor and no angle to it. It was December, people had been good to each other all year, and it seemed like the right way to say so.

I stopped after 2021. Four years was a good run, and I would rather it be remembered as a nice thing that happened than a thing that quietly got worse.

The trade

What it cost

The server worked. That is also the thing that broke it for the people who were there first.

The original group — the ones it was actually built for — slowly stopped showing up. Too many people, too many strangers, too much noise. One of my oldest friends has never liked being in a room with people he does not know, and by then that is precisely what The Game Center was. I spent years pushing to get more people in, which was the same act as pushing my friends out. I did not see it that way at the time.

So I made them a smaller, private one instead. It is still running, and it is the one they actually use.

I do not think either room was a mistake. They just could not be the same room.

The honest thread

On wanting to own things

I said at the top that I built the server because I wanted to own it, and I want to come back to that, because I think it runs through everything on this site.

I like making things I control. Servers, apps, mods, this website. I would not call it a power trip, because I hardly ever use the power — I leave it alone unless something genuinely requires me. But I like having it, and the reason is that I do not entirely trust other people with it. That is why it took me so long to give anybody else the keys.

I have watched what happens when people want to be in charge more than they want the thing to be good. Some of the drama that eventually split the server was exactly that: people left, started their own rooms, abandoned those, started more. In hindsight it was the healthier ending. Everyone got the room they actually wanted, and I got to stop refereeing.

Nine years on

A tenth of a percent

Someone who joined here as a teenager and left for college once told me this was the server he grew up in. That is the part I did not plan and cannot really take credit for. You cannot design that. You can only leave the door open long enough for it to happen to somebody.

I figure I own about a tenth of a percent of a lot of people's brains. Somebody mentions Discord servers, and somewhere out there a person goes: oh, The Game Center — yeah, I used to be in that.

That is a strange thing to have built, and on balance it is the thing I am proudest of. It happened almost entirely because I would not stop asking people to join.

The door is still open

If that invite ever stops working, this one goes to the same place.

The other rooms

There are three more

The Game Center is the oldest of four Discord servers I run. The rest are on the Community page.

Dlonem's Den is the one that goes with the YouTube channel. The other two are for my Crusader Kings III mods — The Long Night & Azor Ahai and Supernatural — where patch notes land first and load-order questions get sorted out before they ruin anyone's campaign.
